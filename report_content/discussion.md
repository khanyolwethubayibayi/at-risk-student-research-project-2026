## Overview of Key Findings

This study set out to establish which factors are associated with first-year academic risk in the Diploma in Civil Engineering at CPUT, and whether the available institutional data could be used to develop a reliable early-warning model. Three key findings emerged from the analysis. First, 36.7% (121 of 330) of First Time Entering (first year) students failed at least one subject, substantiating the persistence of academic underperformance that prompted the study. Second, demographic, financial, and residential variables were not significantly associated with academic risk once multiple testing was accounted for, whereas performance in a small group of technical subjects, led by Theory of Structures, dominated every measure of variable importance. Third, both Logistic Regression and Random Forest demonstrated strong predictive performance when final subject marks were included (ROC-AUC = 0.932 and 0.940, respectively) but performed no better than chance when marks were excluded (ROC-AUC = 0.572 and 0.497). The following sections discuss these findings in light of the literature reviewed in Chapter 2.

## Prevalence of Risk and First-Year Transition

The findings showing that more than a third (36.7%) of the first-year students were classified as at-risk aligns closely with the argument of Connolly et al. (2017) that the first year is a period of difficult transition that can negatively affect academic performance. Similarly, it supports Tinto’s (2019) perspective, as cited by Samoila and Vrabie (2023), that student persistence is strongly influenced by the extent to which students successfully integrate into the academic and social environment of the institution. The at-risk rate differed between cohorts (43.0% in 2024 and 30.0% in 2025), and the 2025 decrease might be interpreted as evidence that interventions are taking effect. However, the relationship between academic year and risk status was not statistically significant (χ² = 3.52, p = 0.319), meaning the decline cannot confidently be attributed to any intervention and may instead reflect ordinary cohort variation. Furthermore, it is important to recognise that at-risk status was defined as failing at least one subject. This intentionally sensitive criterion captures both students experiencing minor academic setbacks and those encountering more substantial academic challenges. As a result, the reported at-risk rate of 36.7% should be interpreted as an indication of vulnerability to academic progression delays rather than a direct measure of potential student dropout.

## Background and Socio-Economic Factors

The three hypotheses concerning student background characteristics, funding, and residence status were not supported by the findings of this study. No statistically significant associations were observed between academic risk and funding status (χ² = 0.002, p = 0.963), residence status (χ² = 0.046, p = 0.831), school quintile (χ² = 6.11, p = 0.191), home language (p = 0.620), ethnic group (p = 0.639), gender (p = 0.731), or previous activity before enrolment (p = 0.267). Although students who enrolled at age 18 years or younger exhibited a substantially lower at-risk rate (17.8%) compared to students in older age categories (37.8% to 40.6%), this association was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons (adjusted p = 0.509).

These findings differ from those of Singell and Waddell (2010), who reported that student characteristics, academic performance, and financial aid collectively influence retention, as well as Goradia and Bugarcic (2019), who found that non-academic factors can contribute additional explanatory value beyond academic indicators. However, this discrepancy should be interpreted cautiously rather than taken as evidence that these factors have no role in shaping student success.

Firstly, the cohort is fairly homogeneous. About 94.2% of students entered with an admission-to-bachelor's matric, 74.8% were NSFAS-funded and 89.4% were African, so admission requirements and funding already operate as filters that restrict the variation these variables are able to explain. Since NSFAS funding is nearly universal in this program, the absence of a funding effect may mean that the principal financial obstacle has been removed for most students rather than that finances are irrelevant. Moreover, a binary funded/not-funded variable cannot capture whether payments are adequate or timely.

Secondly, residence status may be an insufficient measure of the factors that Tinto’s framework identifies as critical for student persistence. Residence placement alone provides limited information regarding the quality of students’ living and study environments, their sense of belonging, or their academic and social integration within the institution. This may help explain the lack of a significant relationship between residence status and academic risk. Likewise, although Bean and Metzner’s attrition model highlights the importance of external environmental influences such as financial pressures and living conditions, these factors were not captured in sufficient depth within the present dataset.

Thirdly, statistical power is limited. With 330 records, and with expected cell counts below five for home language, ethnic group and previous activity, a non-significant test does not constitute evidence of no effect. Descriptive patterns such as the 49.3% at-risk rate among Quintile 2 students or the 57.1% rate among Tshivenda speakers — are therefore better regarded as hypotheses for larger samples than as findings. The same holds for prior labour force experience (OR ≈ 2.3), which rests on only three students.

Although age did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple testing, the lower risk observed among students aged 18 years or younger is broadly consistent with the findings of Kocsis and Molnár (2025), who emphasised the influence of personal characteristics and prior educational experiences on academic outcomes. It also aligns with Connolly et al. (2017), who highlighted the advantages associated with a direct transition from secondary school to higher education. Nevertheless, given the lack of statistical significance after adjustment, this finding should be interpreted as an emerging trend rather than conclusive evidence of an age-related effect on academic risk.

## Subject Level Predictors of Student Academic Risk

Academic performance, particularly performance in a small number of technical subjects, was the dominant determinant of risk. Theory of Structures recorded the highest failure rate in each year from 2022 to 2025 (79 failures, 23.9% overall) and the lowest average mark (roughly 56% to 57%), followed by Engineering Mathematics 1 (10.9%), Surveying (10.6%) and Engineering Drawing 1 (10.6%). The importance of Theory of Structures was reinforced through three independent analytical approaches. It exhibited the strongest protective effect in the logistic regression model (OR ≈ 0.09), received the highest feature importance score in the Random Forest model (0.259), which was more than twice that of the next-ranked predictor, and generated the largest reduction in test-set ROC-AUC during permutation importance analysis (approximately 0.20 for Logistic Regression and 0.14 for Random Forest). This consistency across a parametric model, a tree ensemble and a model-agnostic test lends the finding considerable credibility.

These findings are consistent with previous research identifying academic performance and prior academic achievement as key determinants of student success (Kocsis & Molnár, 2025; Latif et al., 2021). They also support Tinto’s theory of student persistence, which emphasises the importance of academic integration for successful progression. One possible explanation is that Theory of Structures requires competency in mathematics, physics, and mechanics, making it particularly challenging for students with weaker foundational knowledge in these areas. However, the present study could not directly evaluate this explanation, as data on admission point scores and prior school academic achievement were not incorporated. Consequently, it remains unclear whether poor performance in this subject reflects inadequate prior preparation or challenges inherent to the subject itself.

The non-technical subjects had minimal contribution to at-risk classification. Professional and Technical Communication had only four failures, all of which were also failures in Computer Skills, but with so few cases this co-failure pattern is descriptive only. The discrepancy for Applied Mechanics, which ranked second by impurity importance (0.109) but did not feature under permutation importance and had a failure rate of only 1.8%, illustrates a known weakness of impurity-based measures, which can overstate the importance of correlated continuous predictors. The permutation results, computed on unseen test data, are the more reliable of the two. These findings support directing tutoring and academic support towards Theory of Structures, Engineering Mathematics 1, Surveying and Engineering Drawing 1, in keeping with the aim of identifying the subjects that contribute most to risk.

## Predictive Model Performance and the Contribution of Assessment Marks

The comparison between the two feature sets revealed that assessment marks were the primary drivers of predictive performance. Models developed using only demographic, financial, residential, and background variables (Set A) performed no better than chance, with ROC-AUC values of 0.572 for Logistic Regression and 0.497 for Random Forest. In contrast, the inclusion of assessment marks (Set B) substantially improved model performance, increasing accuracy to approximately 86%, recall to 77.8%, and ROC-AUC values to above 0.93 for both algorithms. The non-overlapping confidence intervals between the two feature sets further demonstrate the substantial improvement associated with assessment-related variables.

These results should be interpreted cautiously. Since the at-risk outcome was defined using subject marks (a mark below 50 in at least one subject), the strong performance of Set B (With final assessment marks) largely reflects the models' ability to reproduce the rule used to create the target variable rather than providing a true prediction of future risk. Consequently, these results represent the maximum performance that could be expected from the available data rather than evidence of genuine early identification capability. In contrast, Set A provides a more realistic assessment of early prediction, showing that demographic, financial, residential, and other background factors alone were insufficient for accurately identifying at-risk students. This finding is consistent with studies suggesting that the combination of academic and non-academic variables is more effective than relying on either category independently. It also highlights the need to incorporate additional predictive information, such as secondary school achievement, admission point scores, and learning management system activity, to support earlier and more reliable risk detection.

The finding that assessment results are the strongest aligns with previous research demonstrating the usefulness of academic assessments as early warning indicators of student difficulty. Veerasamy et al. (2020) used early assessment performance as an early warning sign, and Adnan et al. (2021) examined prediction at different course stages to balance earliness against accuracy. The key difference is timing, those studies use assessments available before the outcome is known, whereas the final marks used here are available only at the end of the year, limiting their practical value for early intervention. Replacing semester assessments (first tests and assignments) for final marks would convert the Set B approach into a true early-warning model and allow direct testing of the trade-off identified by Singell and Waddell (2010): whether at-risk students can be identified early enough to help.

Differences between the two modelling approaches were minimal. Although Random Forest achieved marginally higher accuracy (86.9% vs. 85.9%) and ROC-AUC (0.940 vs. 0.932) than Logistic Regression, both models achieved the same recall (0.778), and their ROC-AUC confidence intervals overlapped substantially. These results indicate that neither algorithm can be considered statistically superior for identifying at-risk students. This finding is consistent with previous reviews that have reported comparable effectiveness of Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and Random Forests in educational risk prediction (Nik Nurul Hafzan et al., 2019; Córdova-Esparza et al., 2025).

Given their similar predictive performance, Logistic Regression offers the practical advantage of interpretability, as its coefficients provide clear insight into the factors associated with student risk. Hyperparameter tuning yielded little additional benefit. The tuned Logistic Regression model (C = 0.1) achieved a slightly lower ROC-AUC (0.915), while the tuned Random Forest model (maximum depth = 8) maintained similar accuracy but shifted the balance from recall (0.778 to 0.750) towards precision (0.848 to 0.871). These results suggest that the default model settings were already close to optimal for the available training sample of 231 records. Because the primary objective of the study was to maximise the identification of at-risk students, the default Random Forest configuration may be preferred operationally due to its slightly higher discrimination while maintaining recall comparable to Logistic Regression. However, where model transparency and ease of interpretation are important considerations, Logistic Regression remains an attractive alternative.

## Implications for an Early-Warning System and Limitations

Both models failed to identify 8 of the 36 at-risk students in the test set, resulting in a false negative rate of 22.2%. In addition, Logistic Regression produced 6 false positives, while Random Forest produced 5. From an institutional perspective, false negatives are of greater concern because students who are incorrectly classified as not at risk would not receive targeted academic support, whereas false positives would merely result in some students receiving support that may not be strictly necessary. This distinction highlights the importance of prioritising recall when developing student risk prediction models.

The findings of Connolly et al. (2017) suggest that academic performance can be improved when at-risk students are identified early and provided with structured interventions. Similarly, Magd et al. (2022) emphasised the value of integrating early warning systems with academic advising, tutoring, and peer-support initiatives. Consequently, the models developed in this study should be viewed primarily as screening tools that assist institutions in directing support services towards students who are most likely to experience academic difficulties during the early stages of their studies. Furthermore, lowering the classification threshold may improve recall and reduce the number of missed at-risk students, although this would come at the expense of an increased number of false positives.

## Summary

Overall, the evidence suggests that first-year (First Time Entering students) risk in the Diploma in Civil Engineering is concentrated in a small number of technical subjects rather than in any readily identifiable demographic or financial group within this cohort. Once assessment marks are available, both models classify risk accurately and perform similarly, yet the same data cannot identify at-risk students at the point of admission. Their usefulness for early intervention will thus hinge on incorporating information that is available early such as semester assessment results, prior academic achievement and learning management system activity which aligns with the literature's view of predictive modelling as a way of enabling timely, targeted support (Nik Nurul Hafzan et al., 2019; Magd et al., 2022).
