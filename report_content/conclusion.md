## Conclusion

This study examined the factors associated with academic risk among First Time Entering first-year students in the Diploma in Civil Engineering at CPUT and evaluated whether Logistic Regression and Random Forest could identify at-risk students. Of the 330 students analysed, 36.7% failed at least one subject. Academic performance emerged as the dominant determinant of risk in both models, and in particular performance in Theory of Structures, which had the highest failure rate (23.9%) and the strongest effect across the odds ratios, impurity importance and permutation importance. Engineering Mathematics 1, Surveying and Engineering Drawing 1 formed a second tier of influential subjects. In contrast, funding status, residence status, gender, ethnic group, home language, school quintile and previous activity showed no statistically significant association with risk, and the hypotheses that financial and residential circumstances influence performance were therefore not supported in this cohort.

With the inclusion of final assessment marks, both Logistic Regression and Random Forest achieved strong predictive performance, with accuracies of 85.9% and 86.9%, ROC-AUC values of 0.932 and 0.940, and identical recall rates of 77.8%. The substantial overlap in their confidence intervals indicates that neither model demonstrated a clear performance advantage over the other. In contrast, models developed without assessment marks performed no better than chance, highlighting the limited predictive value of the available demographic, financial, residential, and background variables. Because academic risk was defined directly from subject marks, the strong performance of the models with assessment data largely reflects their ability to reproduce the classification rule rather than genuinely predict future risk. Consequently, the models cannot reliably identify at-risk students at the point of admission. Furthermore, both models failed to identify 8 of the 36 at-risk students in the test set (22.2%), representing an important practical limitation for intervention purposes. The findings therefore suggest that the current data are insufficient for effective early identification and that a meaningful early-warning system will require the inclusion of indicators available earlier in the academic year, such as prior academic achievement, semester assessment results, and student engagement data.

## Recommendations

Support should be targeted first at the subjects most strongly associated with risk (Theory of Structures, Engineering Mathematics 1, Surveying and Engineering Drawing 1). Tutors should be appointed early in the first semester, and the availability of tutoring and academic advising should be communicated clearly so that students know where to turn for help. Advising should also assist students with workload management and study strategies, consistent with the early, structured support shown by Connolly et al. (2017) to improve performance and recommended by Magd et al. (2022).

To enhance the practical usefulness of the predictive model, future implementations should replace final assessment marks with in-semester assessment indicators, such as early tests, assignments, and class assessments. This would allow students at risk of academic failure to be identified while there is still sufficient time for meaningful intervention. Furthermore, the classification threshold could be adjusted to increase recall and reduce the number of missed at-risk students, even if this results in a higher number of false positives. The predictive model should therefore be used as a decision-support tool to prioritise students for assistance rather than as a standalone mechanism for determining intervention. Before operational deployment, the model should also be validated using data from a new student cohort to confirm its generalisability and robustness.

Future research should add high school results and admission point scores to show how prior performance relates to first-year outcomes, together with learning management system data (submissions, platform usage and time spent per subject) and tutoring and consultation records, to examine engagement and the effect of interventions. Because funding and residence were not significant using only binary indicators, richer measures, such as the adequacy and timeliness of funding and the quality of the study environment, could also be explored. Finally, the reduction in the at-risk rate in 2025 was not statistically significant, so any support introduced should be formally evaluated to establish whether it works.

## Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the research problem, certain limitations should be acknowledged. These limitations include study scope, methodological constraints, data availability as well as external factors that may affect the findings. Acknowledging these limitations is crucial for ensuring transparency, providing context for interpreting the results, and identifying opportunities for future research to build upon and strengthen the current study.

Important predictors identified in previous research, such as high school academic performance and Admission Point Scores (APS), were not available and therefore could not be examined. Likewise, no information was available on student engagement and learning behaviours, including learning management system activity, attendance, participation, and interactions with academic support services such as tutoring, mentoring, and consultation programmes. No available data addresses student attitude and engagement. This would assist in identifying the effect of student’s attitude and engagement in school activity against performance. These factors may play a significant role in shaping academic outcomes and could improve the predictive ability of future models.

The modelling and statistical analyses were also limited by sample size. The dataset contained 330 student records, and the test set only 99 students, of whom 36 were at risk, so performance estimates were based on a single train-test split and are sensitive to small changes in classification. Several chi-square tests had expected cell counts below five (home language, ethnic group and previous activity), and some categories were very small (for example, three students from the labour force), so non-significant results should not be taken as evidence of no effect. The study covers one program at one institution across four cohorts, which limits generalization to other programs and institutions. The analyses identify associations and do not establish causation
