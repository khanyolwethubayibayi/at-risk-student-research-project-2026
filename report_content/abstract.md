Low pass rates among first-year students in the Diploma in Civil Engineering at the Cape Peninsula University of Technology (CPUT) create a need to identify factors influencing students at risk of academic failure early enough to support them. This study examined which socio-economic, financial, residential and academic factors are associated with academic risk among First Time Entering (first-year) students, and whether Logistic Regression and Random Forest can identify at-risk students at registration or only once marks are known. Registration, funding, residence and academic records for 330 students (2022 to 2025) were analyzed, with a student classified as at risk if they obtained below 50% in at least one of the ten first-year subjects.

Exploratory analysis and chi-square tests were followed by model development on a stratified 70/30 split (test set n = 99), comparing models without (Set A) and with (Set B) final subject marks. Overall, 36.7% of students were at risk. Funding, residence, gender, ethnicity, home language, school quintile and previous activity were not significantly associated with risk, whereas performance in technical subjects, particularly Theory of Structures, was the strongest determinant in both models. Without marks, neither model performed better than chance (ROC-AUC = 0.57 and 0.50). With marks, Logistic Regression and Random Forest achieved ROC-AUC values of 0.93 and 0.94 and a recall of 77.8%, with no meaningful difference between them, although 8 of the 36 at-risk test students were missed.

In answer to the research problem, academic risk in this cohort is explained by early performance in key technical subjects rather than by background characteristics, and registration data alone cannot identify at-risk students; accurate identification was possible only once marks were known. Because risk was defined from the same marks, this performance represents an upper bound and not true early prediction. The study contributes (i) evidence from a first-year Civil Engineering diploma cohort that demographic, financial and residential factors do not independently explain academic risk, (ii) a direct comparison of models with and without marks that shows the limits of registration data for early warning, and (iii) a basis for an in-semester early-warning system. It recommends early tutoring in high-risk subjects and the use of in-semester assessments, prior academic achievement and learning management system data to support early identification.
